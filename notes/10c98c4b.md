## What it is (three statements, each machine-checked)

**1. The core B says exactly that · is orthogonal to its transpose, and ∘ is its decoder.**
B = {(x·y)∘(y·x) = x, (x∘y)·(y∘x) = x}. Let φ(x,y) = (x·y, y·x) and τ(x,y) = (y,x). Then φτ = τφ.
- B says ψφ = id and φψ = id for ψ(u,v) = (u∘v, v∘u), so φ is a bijection of Q².
- Conversely, if φ is a bijection, then φ⁻¹ also commutes with τ, so φ⁻¹(u,v) = (D(u,v), D(v,u)) for one operation
  D. Setting ∘ = D satisfies B.

So the models of B are exactly the groupoids (Q,·) orthogonal to their transpose (x,y) ↦ y·x, and ∘ is determined
by ·. B is symmetric in the two operations: · is equally the decoder of ∘. Two easy consequences of B alone:
x·y = y·x implies x = y, and x·x = y·y implies x = y (both because φ is injective).

**This statement is known.** Bonatto, Kinyon, Stanovský and Vojtěchovský, "Involutive latin solutions of the
Yang-Baxter equation" ([arXiv:1910.02148](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02148), §2.5, read in full via ar5iv; published
in J. Algebra 565 (2021) 128–159 according to our lookup):
- They call a binary algebra *Δ-bijective* when (x,y) ↦ (xy, yx) is a bijection.
- Lemma 2.10: the inverse is Δ of a second operation ∗. That is our ∘, and its two components are B.
- Lemma 2.11: every Δ-bijective algebra is uniquely 2-divisible, which is our "x·x = y·y ⇒ x = y" with a square
  root x∗x.
- They say the idea comes from Rump, "A decomposition theorem for square-free unitary solutions of the quantum
  Yang-Baxter equation", Adv. Math. 193 (2005) 40–55. There, Δ-bijectivity is the non-degeneracy condition for
  cycle sets.
- Our lookup also reports that Zhang and Du (arXiv:2212.00274, Lemma 2.10) call ∘ the *dual operation* and note
  that it is unique. Dehornoy et al., *Foundations of Garside Theory* (EMS 2015), Ch. XIII, is cited for the same
  fact. We read neither.

Their setting is left quasigroups: cycle sets and *rumples*, in which every left translation is a bijection.

**An earlier statement of the same fact** (added after a second lookup; the key statement checked by us):
- Dehornoy, "Set-theoretic solutions of the Yang–Baxter equation, RC-calculus, and Garside germs", Adv. Math. 282
  (2015), [arXiv:1403.3019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1403.3019), §1, read via ar5iv.
  - An RC-system is "bijective" when (s,t) ↦ (s∗t, t∗s) is a bijection of S×S. The term is credited to Rump, who
    calls RC-systems "cycloids".
  - The lemma after Definition 1.6 (Lemma 1.7, according to our lookup) shows that the "involutivity laws" (1.8)
    hold exactly when that map is a bijection whose inverse is (s,t) ↦ (s∗̃t, t∗̃s), for a unique ∗̃. Rewritten
    with x∘y := y∗̃x, these are the laws of B.
  - His examples beyond left quasigroups are trivial: s∗t = f(t) with f a permutation.
- According to the lookup, which read the authors' PDF: Dehornoy, Digne, Godelle, Krammer and Michel, *Foundations
  of Garside Theory* (EMS 2015), Ch. XIII, Lemma 1.28, has the same lemma, with the same scope.

So the prior art for B goes back at least to 2015 (Dehornoy, after Rump 2005). Every source we saw combines it with
the right-cyclic law or with bijective translations.

**Not a Yang–Baxter solution** (part 7 of the script). In none of the 16 non-degenerate models on 4 and 5 elements
does r(x,y) = (x·y, y·x) satisfy the braid relation. Positive controls do: the 2×2 rectangular band and the flip
x·y = y. So the theory uses Δ-bijectivity outside the Yang–Baxter setting where it was studied.

Check (part 4 of the script): all 3⁹ tables on 3 elements. 288 of them are orthogonal to their transpose. They give
62 models of B up to isomorphism, 38 of them non-degenerate. Mace4 gives the same 62 and 38. **Correction:** the
dossier, `literature.json` and commit `2255b8e` say 40 non-degenerate models of B on 3 elements; the right number is
38.

**2. Under B, each absorption law is a one-operation law: idempotent translations.** E proves, both ways, under B
(part 1):
- (x∘y)·x = x ⟺ x·(x·y) = x·y
- x·(y∘x) = x ⟺ (x·y)·y = x·y
- (x·y)∘x = x ⟺ x∘(x∘y) = x∘y
- x∘(y·x) = x ⟺ (x∘y)∘y = x∘y

The hand proof of the first: write x = a·b and y = b·a. Then x∘y = a by B, and the law becomes a·(a·b) = a·b.

So `10c98c4b` is equivalent to: **· is orthogonal to its transpose, and every left and every right translation of ·
and of its decoder ∘ is idempotent** (L_x² = L_x, R_y² = R_y). E proves this equivalence both ways (part 2), and
also proves that both operations are idempotent. None of the four translation laws follows from B and the other
three (E saturates on each).

This places the theory at the opposite end from both known settings. In a self-orthogonal Latin square every
translation is bijective; in a rumple every left translation is. Here every translation is idempotent, and an
idempotent bijection is the identity. So no non-degenerate model is a left (or right) quasigroup. If every left
translation of · were bijective, · would be the right projection x·y = y. Its decoder would then be the right
projection too, so ∘ = ·.

**3. With x∘y = x·y the theory has rectangular bands as models, but it is wider than them.** Identifying the
operations gives S = {(xy)(yx) = x, (xy)x = xx, x(yx) = xx}: Schröder's second law plus two absorptions. Rectangular
bands satisfy S (E). S implies idempotence, but not (xy)z = xz or x(yz) = xz (E saturates, part 3). In a
non-degenerate model, · is never a rectangular band: a rectangular band satisfies (xy)(yx) = x, so it would be its
own decoder and ∘ = · would follow.

## Small models (Mace4, complete searches, part 6)

- Non-degenerate models up to isomorphism: none on 2 or 3 elements, 2 on 4, 14 on 5. These counts agree with the
  dossier. Size 6 has at least 214; that search stopped at its cap.
- On 4 elements, one model satisfies x(yz) = xz in both operations, and the other satisfies (xy)z = xz in both. It
  can't be one model with both laws, since that would make · a rectangular band. This is consistent with the two
  being mirror images. We did not check it with canonical forms.
- On 5 elements, 7 of the 14 models satisfy x(yx) = x and (xy)x = x. None satisfies x(yz) = xz.
- No law from a short list holds in every non-degenerate model on 4 and 5 elements. The list covered x(yz) = xz,
  (xy)z = xz, associativity, x(yx) = x, (xy)x = x and Schröder, in either operation. So the small models give no
  hint of a missing structural law.

## Literature

What was read, and how:

- **Δ-bijective algebras** (Rump 2005; Bonatto, Kinyon, Stanovský and Vojtěchovský): see statement 1. This is the
  prior art for B.
- **Schröder's second law.** A Schröder quasigroup is a quasigroup with xy·yx = x (Shcherbacov,
  [arXiv:2206.12844](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.12844), Def. 7; read in full via ar5iv).
  - Shcherbacov's Lemma 1 is our "xy = yx ⇒ x = y", proved there for quasigroups. He writes that some of these
    identities "guarantee that a quasigroup is orthogonal to its parastrophe", citing Belousov.
  - Existence: idempotent Schröder quasigroups of order n exist if and only if n ≡ 0, 1 (mod 4), n ≠ 5, 9. Sources:
    Bennett, Wei and Zhu, *Aequationes Math.* 51 (1996) 100–114, Zbl 0844.05027, completing Colbourn and Stinson
    1988; Lindner, Mendelsohn and Sun, *Discrete Math.* 32 (1980) 271–280. Review read for Bennett, Wei and Zhu
    only.
  - Further work, from titles and reviews only: holey Schröder designs (Bennett and Wei 1997); a specified number of
    idempotents (Bennett and Zhang 2012); palindromic quasigroups (J. D. H. Smith 2014, Zbl 1293.05026); n-ary
    Schröder quasigroups (Stojaković and Tasić 2000, Zbl 0976.20052).
  - Our models are idempotent on 5 and 6 elements, orders where no idempotent Schröder quasigroup exists. There is
    no conflict, since · is not a quasigroup here.
- **Belousov, *Parastrophic-orthogonal quasigroups*.** Translation of the 1983 preprint, *Quasigroups Related
  Systems* 13 (2005) 25–72, Zbl 1106.20050; we read the review only.
  - According to the review, identities A^α(x, A^β(x, A^γ(x,y))) = y between parastrophes of a quasigroup operation
    force orthogonal parastrophes. These fall into seven types, minimal in variables (2) and occurrences (5).
  - The setting is quasigroups throughout: parastrophes other than the transpose need the divisions.
- **Self-orthogonal groupoids.** Syrbu, "About separable self-orthogonal n-operations" (*Bul. Acad. Ştiinţe Repub.
  Mold. Mat.* 1994, no. 3, 37–42, Zbl 0858.20059), speaks of "self-orthogonal n-ary groupoids" for n ≥ 3, so the
  notion exists for groupoids, not only quasigroups. For n = 2 it would read "orthogonal to its transpose". Review
  only.
- **Orthogonality of arbitrary binary operations.** Mullen and Shcherbacov, "On orthogonality of binary operations
  and squares" (*Bul. Acad. Ştiinţe Repub. Mold. Mat.* 2005, no. 2, 3–42; authors checked on mathnet.ru, basm125),
  treat orthogonality for groupoids, left quasigroups and quasigroups. That is from a search snippet; the PDF did not
  load as text. (An earlier draft of this memo wrongly gave Belyavskaya as the author.)
- **Conjugate orthogonality.** Bennett, *Can. J. Math.* 41 (1989) 341–368, as read by our lookup: a (2,1,3)-conjugate
  orthogonal quasigroup "is more commonly called self-orthogonal". The term "short conjugate-orthogonal identities"
  is Evans's. Our lookup also reports that Scerbacova and Shcherbacov (arXiv:1509.00796) list Schröder's second law
  among Belousov's seven identities. That it gives orthogonality to the transpose, (2,1,3), is our inference from
  statement 1; no source we saw states it.
- **Evans.** No full text was accessible. The bibliography was checked by our lookup (zbMATH, EuDML):
  - "Latin cubes orthogonal to their transposes — a ternary analogue of Stein quasigroups", *Aequationes Math.* 9
    (1973) 296–297;
  - "Algebraic structures associated with latin squares and orthogonal arrays", *Congr. Numer.* 13 (1975) 31–52;
  - "The construction of orthogonal k-skeins and latin k-cubes", *Aequationes Math.* 14 (1976) 485–492;
  - "Universal algebra and Euler's officer problem", *Amer. Math. Monthly* 86 (1979) 466–473;
  - "Latin squares and universal algebra", *Ann. Discrete Math.* 46 (1991) 203–216.

  Not confirmed: whether Evans writes orthogonality with decoder operations as identities, and whether he allows
  non-quasigroups.
- **ETP** (one operation, ∘ = ·): Equation 159 is x = (x◇y)◇(y◇x), Schröder's second law. Equations 323 and 378 are
  the two translation laws. This agrees with the ETP numbers in the dossier.
- **Self-orthogonal Latin squares.** They exist for every order except 2, 3 and 6 (Brayton, Coppersmith and Hoffman,
  *Bull. AMS* 80 (1974)). This is the Latin end of the picture; ours is the opposite end.

## Verdict (draft)

- **The core B is known.** It is exactly a Δ-bijective binary algebra together with its dual operation (Dehornoy 2015,
  the lemma after Definition 1.6; Foundations of Garside Theory, Lemma XIII.1.28; Bonatto, Kinyon, Stanovský and
  Vojtěchovský, Lemma 2.10; idea from Rump 2005). The search rediscovered it. With ∘ = ·, B is
  Schröder's second law, a well-studied quasigroup identity with a known spectrum. Orthogonality of operations that
  are not quasigroups is also studied (Mullen and Shcherbacov 2005; Syrbu 1994).
- **What we found no source for:** the full theory, Δ-bijective groupoids in which every translation of the
  operation and of its dual is idempotent.
  - The Δ-bijective literature we saw works with the right-cyclic law or with bijective left translations: cycle
    sets, rumples, RC-systems.
  - No non-degenerate model of this theory is a left or right quasigroup, and none of its small models is a
    Yang–Baxter solution.
  - A second lookup (22 Nov, morning) also found nothing in the idempotent and degenerate Yang–Baxter literature
    (Lebed 2017; Stanovský and Vojtěchovský 2021; Colazzo, Jespers, Kubat, Van Antwerpen and Verwimp 2024, abstracts
    only). Those papers assume left non-degeneracy or r² = r.
  - Not read: Rump 2005 (paywalled). One snippet-only lead: J. Dudek, "On varieties of groupoid modes",
    Demonstratio Math. 27 (1994), may list (xy)(yx) = x among idempotent medial identities.
- **Would a universal algebraist care?** Possibly, mildly. The characterization in statement 2 is clean, and the
  model counts (0, 0, 2, 14, ≥ 214) suggest a real class rather than a degenerate one. The open questions an
  algebraist would ask first:
  - a structure theorem for the models;
  - the spectrum (is every n ≥ 4 realised?);
  - whether the free algebra on two generators is finite (the profile did not close it: 3,714 elements on the models
    at our depth).
- **Not yet:** calling it new. That needs the reading above and, ideally, a question to people who work on
  Δ-bijectivity and orthogonality: the Prague–Denver group behind the rumple paper, who also use Mace4, or the
  Chişinău school. Whether to ask is the user's decision.
