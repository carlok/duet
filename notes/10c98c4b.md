    x·(y∘x) = x·x      x∘(y·x) = x∘x      (x∘y)·x = x·x      (x·y)∘x = x∘x

## What it is (three statements, each machine-checked)

**1. The core B says exactly that · is orthogonal to its transpose, and ∘ is its decoder.**
B = {(x·y)∘(y·x) = x, (x∘y)·(y∘x) = x}. Let φ(x,y) = (x·y, y·x) and τ(x,y) = (y,x). Then φτ = τφ.
- B says ψφ = id and φψ = id for ψ(u,v) = (u∘v, v∘u), so φ is a bijection of Q².
- Conversely, if φ is a bijection, then φ⁻¹ also commutes with τ, so φ⁻¹(u,v) = (D(u,v), D(v,u)) for one operation
  D. Setting ∘ = D satisfies B.

So the models of B are exactly the groupoids (Q,·) orthogonal to their transpose (x,y) ↦ y·x, and ∘ is determined
by ·. B is symmetric in the two operations: · is equally the decoder of ∘. Two easy consequences of B alone:
x·y = y·x implies x = y, and x·x = y·y implies x = y (both because φ is injective).

Check (part 4 of the script): all 3⁹ tables on 3 elements. 288 of them are orthogonal to their transpose. They give
62 models of B up to isomorphism, 38 of them non-degenerate. Mace4 gives the same 62 and 38. **Correction:** the
dossier, `literature.json` and commit `2255b8e` say 40 non-degenerate models of B on 3 elements; the right number is
38.

**2. Under B, each absorption law is a one-operation law: idempotent translations.** E proves, both ways, under B
(part 1):
- (x∘y)·x = x ⟺ x·(x·y) = x·y
- x·(y∘x) = x ⟺ (x·y)·y = x·y
- (x·y)∘x = x ⟺ x∘(x∘y) = x∘y
- x∘(y·x) = x ⟺ (x∘y)∘y = x∘y

The hand proof of the first: write x = a·b and y = b·a. Then x∘y = a by B, and the law becomes a·(a·b) = a·b.

So `10c98c4b` is equivalent to: **· is orthogonal to its transpose, and every left and every right translation of ·
and of its decoder ∘ is idempotent** (L_x² = L_x, R_y² = R_y). E proves this equivalence both ways (part 2), and
also proves that both operations are idempotent. None of the four translation laws follows from B and the other
three (E saturates on each).

This places the theory at the opposite end from the classical case. A self-orthogonal Latin square has all of its
translations bijective. Here they are all idempotent, and a translation that is both is the identity. So in a
model with more than one element, · is never a quasigroup: if every translation were bijective, x·y = y and
x·y = x would both hold.

**3. With x∘y = x·y the theory has rectangular bands as models, but it is wider than them.** Identifying the
operations gives S = {(xy)(yx) = x, (xy)x = xx, x(yx) = xx}: Schröder's second law plus two absorptions. Rectangular
bands satisfy S (E). S implies idempotence, but not (xy)z = xz or x(yz) = xz (E saturates, part 3). In a
non-degenerate model, · is never a rectangular band: a rectangular band satisfies (xy)(yx) = x, so it would be its
own decoder and ∘ = · would follow.

## Small models (Mace4, complete searches, part 6)

- Non-degenerate models up to isomorphism: none on 2 or 3 elements, 2 on 4, 14 on 5. These counts agree with the
  dossier. Size 6 has at least 214; that search stopped at its cap.
- On 4 elements, one model satisfies x(yz) = xz in both operations, and the other satisfies (xy)z = xz in both. It
  can't be one model with both laws, since that would make · a rectangular band. This is consistent with the two
  being mirror images. We did not check it with canonical forms.
- On 5 elements, 7 of the 14 models satisfy x(yx) = x and (xy)x = x. None satisfies x(yz) = xz.
- No law from a short list holds in every non-degenerate model on 4 and 5 elements. The list covered x(yz) = xz,
  (xy)z = xz, associativity, x(yx) = x, (xy)x = x and Schröder, in either operation. So the small models give no
  hint of a missing structural law.

## Literature

What was read, and how:

- **Schröder's second law.** A Schröder quasigroup is a quasigroup with xy·yx = x (Shcherbacov,
  [arXiv:2206.12844](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.12844), Def. 7; read in full via ar5iv).
  - Shcherbacov's Lemma 1 is our "xy = yx ⇒ x = y", proved there for quasigroups. He writes that some of these
    identities "guarantee that a quasigroup is orthogonal to its parastrophe", citing Belousov.
  - Existence: idempotent Schröder quasigroups of order n exist if and only if n ≡ 0, 1 (mod 4), n ≠ 5, 9. Sources:
    Bennett, Wei and Zhu, *Aequationes Math.* 51 (1996) 100–114, Zbl 0844.05027, completing Colbourn and Stinson
    1988; Lindner, Mendelsohn and Sun, *Discrete Math.* 32 (1980) 271–280. Review read for Bennett, Wei and Zhu
    only.
  - Further work, from titles and reviews only: holey Schröder designs (Bennett and Wei 1997); a specified number of
    idempotents (Bennett and Zhang 2012); palindromic quasigroups (J. D. H. Smith 2014, Zbl 1293.05026); n-ary
    Schröder quasigroups (Stojaković and Tasić 2000, Zbl 0976.20052).
  - Our models are idempotent on 5 and 6 elements, orders where no idempotent Schröder quasigroup exists. There is
    no conflict, since · is not a quasigroup here.
- **Belousov, *Parastrophic-orthogonal quasigroups*.** Translation of the 1983 preprint, *Quasigroups Related
  Systems* 13 (2005) 25–72, Zbl 1106.20050; we read the review only.
  - According to the review, identities A^α(x, A^β(x, A^γ(x,y))) = y between parastrophes of a quasigroup operation
    force orthogonal parastrophes. These fall into seven types, minimal in variables (2) and occurrences (5).
  - The setting is quasigroups throughout: parastrophes other than the transpose need the divisions.
- **Self-orthogonal groupoids.** Syrbu, "About separable self-orthogonal n-operations" (*Bul. Acad. Ştiinţe Repub.
  Mold. Mat.* 1994, no. 3, 37–42, Zbl 0858.20059), speaks of "self-orthogonal n-ary groupoids" for n ≥ 3, so the
  notion exists for groupoids, not only quasigroups. For n = 2 it would read "orthogonal to its transpose". Review
  only.
- **Orthogonality of arbitrary binary operations.** Belyavskaya's survey "On orthogonality of binary operations and
  squares" (*Bul. Acad. Ştiinţe Repub. Mold. Mat.* 2005, no. 2, 3–42) treats orthogonality for groupoids, left
  quasigroups and quasigroups (search snippet). Not read: the PDF did not load as text.
- **Evans.** Not checked yet. A lookup of Evans's papers on orthogonality as identities (1975, 1976, 1979) is
  under way, and this entry will be replaced with its result.
- **Self-orthogonal Latin squares.** They exist for every order except 2, 3 and 6 (Brayton, Coppersmith and Hoffman,
  *Bull. AMS* 80 (1974)). This is the Latin end of the picture; ours is the opposite end.

Not read, and needed before any claim: Belyavskaya 2005 (full text); Belousov 2005 (full text, for whether any
statement covers groupoids); Evans (see above); Bennett and Zhu, "Conjugate-orthogonal Latin squares and related
structures" (*Contemporary Design Theory*, Wiley 1992); Syrbu 1994.

## Verdict (draft)

- **The core B is known in substance.** "A groupoid orthogonal to its transpose" is a special case of orthogonality
  of binary operations, a notion studied for groupoids, not only quasigroups (Belyavskaya; Syrbu's self-orthogonal
  n-ary groupoids). With ∘ = ·, B is Schröder's second law, a well-studied quasigroup identity with a known
  spectrum. Writing the decoder as a second operation is the standard way to make orthogonality equational (see
  Evans above).
- **What we found no source for:**
  1. B as a two-operation variety in which each operation is the other's decoder, for groupoids.
  2. Its combination with idempotent translations: transpose-orthogonality at the "anti-Latin" end, where no
     translation is bijective unless it is the identity.

  Neither looks like a named structure.
- **Would a universal algebraist care?** Possibly, mildly. The characterization in statement 2 is clean, and the
  model counts (0, 0, 2, 14, ≥ 214) suggest a real class rather than a degenerate one. The open questions an
  algebraist would ask first:
  - a structure theorem for the models;
  - the spectrum (is every n ≥ 4 realised?);
  - whether the free algebra on two generators is finite (the profile did not close it: 3,714 elements on the models
    at our depth).
- **Not yet:** calling it new. That needs the reading above and, ideally, a question to people who work on
  orthogonality of operations (the Chişinău school, Bennett, Zhang). Whether to ask is the user's decision.
