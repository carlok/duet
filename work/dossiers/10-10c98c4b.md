## Literature check

**Transpose-orthogonality with a decoder, plus absorption.** E proves both directions of the equivalence with B ∪ {(x∘y)·x = x, x·(y∘x) = x, (x·y)∘x = x, x∘(y·x) = x}, where B = {(x·y)∘(y·x) = x, (x∘y)·(y∘x) = x}. B says that φ(x,y) = (x·y, y·x) is a bijection of A² whose inverse is (u,v) ↦ (u∘v, v∘u). So · is orthogonal to its transpose as a binary operation, and ∘ decodes the pair. B alone does not imply idempotence or the absorptions (E saturates), so the absorptions are what the search added. For Latin squares, orthogonality to the transpose is self-orthogonality. Self-orthogonal Latin squares exist for every order except 2, 3 and 6 (Brayton, Coppersmith and Hoffman, Bull. AMS 80 (1974)). Here · is never Latin in a non-degenerate model: the theory proves x·(x·y) = x·y, so a bijective left translation would be the identity, and · would be the right projection. With ∘ = ·, B becomes Schröder's second law (xy)(yx) = x. According to the search results, that is one of Belousov's identities that make a quasigroup orthogonal to a parastrophe; we did not read the sources. Models up to isomorphism (Mace4): none on 2 or 3 elements, 2 on 4, 14 on 5. In the smallest one, x·y keeps the high bit of y, and its low bit is x₀ + x₁(1 + y₁) (elements as bit pairs). E saturates against associativity and commutativity of either operation. Searches (22 Nov 2026) found no source for B as a two-operation variety, nor for B with these absorptions.
